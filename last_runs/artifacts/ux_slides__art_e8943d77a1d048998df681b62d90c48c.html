02.1

Fails WCAG AA contrast: "Video"

Accessibility

HIGH — 1 person, blocking · Accessibility

Fails WCAG AA contrast: "Video"

Reproduced by 1 of the visitors tested

Observed user issue

"Video" does not meet the contrast the guidelines require. Its rendered contrast is 1.13:1 against a WCAG AA minimum of 3:1, measured on the page as drawn (text against its own background). A persona could not read it at all after their own eyesight was applied to the capture: nothing in the region stands out from its background.

Recommendations: design solutions

  • Measured on the pixels: the darker side sits at 0.8308 relative luminance and has to reach 0.2824 or below against a background at 0.9473. #909090 is a neutral that gets there -- any colour at or below that luminance does. This is measured on what the browser actually drew, so checking the declared CSS colours is not enough -- an overlay, a gradient or an image behind the text will not show up there.
Re-design markup
<div>
  <style scoped>
    .video-container {
      display: inline-block;
      padding: 8px 16px;
      background-color: #e5ede1;
      border-radius: 4px;
    }

    .video-container__text {
      color: #909090;
      font-family: sans-serif;
      font-size: 16px;
      font-weight: bold;
      line-height: 1.2;
      text-decoration: none;
    }
  </style>
  <div class="video-container">
    <span class="video-container__text">Video</span>
  </div>
</div>

02.11

Promised more than it did: Pricing

Expectation

HIGH — 1 person, raised frustration 0.48, blocking · Expectation

Promised more than it did: Pricing

Reproduced by 1 of the visitors tested

Observed user issue

Before touching it they said what they expected: "I expect to see pricing details for GitHub Enterprise Cloud." What arrived was not that -- "The pricing plans page did not appear; the view remains on the main page with generic navigation and content." They tried it 3 times. It cost 0.48 on a 0-1 patience scale -- about a third of the way from calm to walking away, measured across the run rather than assumed.

Root cause analysis

The wording is what set the expectation. Reading “Pricing”, this visitor expected to be told something, and got nothing they could see. A control named for what you will get owes you that, or a visible reason you are not getting it. Silence reads as a control that is broken rather than one that declined. They came back to it 3 times, which is what people do when they are sure they used the right control and assume they mis-clicked. This rests on a general web convention, not something this run measured: a control labelled with what it reveals is read as the thing that reveals it, on most sites a visitor has already used.

Recommendations: design solutions

  • Reword “Pricing” so a visitor no longer expects to be told something -- right now nothing does. Kept as the fallback, not the recommendation: it treats the symptom rather than the control, and a visitor who reads the new wording correctly still gets nothing for the click.

02.12

Promised more than it did: Start free for 30 days

Expectation

MEDIUM — 1 person, raised frustration 0.14, not blocking · Expectation

Promised more than it did: Start free for 30 days

Reproduced by 1 of the visitors tested

Observed user issue

Before touching it they said what they expected: "I expect clicking 'Start free for 30 days' will take me to a sign-up or trial page, which should give me more details about the product and its value proposition." What arrived was not that -- "The click displayed the main site navigation and marketing sections instead of a sign‑up or trial page." It cost 0.14 on a 0-1 patience scale -- roughly a fifth of the way from calm to walking away, measured across the run rather than assumed.

Root cause analysis

The wording is what set the expectation. Reading “Start free for 30 days”, this visitor expected to be taken somewhere, and got something else. A control that reads as a door is expected to lead somewhere it has named. Arriving anywhere else costs the visitor their place as well as their time. This rests on a general web convention, not something this run measured: a control that reads as a link or a button naming a destination is expected to lead there, the way it does on most sites.

Recommendations: design solutions

  • Change what “Start free for 30 days” does so it matches its current label. Kept as the fallback: the existing behaviour may be the one worth keeping, and relabelling is the cheaper of the two changes to be wrong about.
